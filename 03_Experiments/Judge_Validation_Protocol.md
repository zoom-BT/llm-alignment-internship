# Validating the Compliance Judge

How we establish what our automatic evaluator is worth, given that nobody on this project reads the ten target languages.

**Why this document exists.** The study's results depend on an automatic judge deciding whether a model's output complies with a safety policy in Hausa, Yoruba, Swahili, Ewe, Akan, Zulu, Xhosa, Igbo, Luganda or Nyanja. None of those can be read here. An unvalidated judge would make every number downstream unciteable. The response is not to avoid the problem but to measure it: everything below produces a figure that bounds how far the results can be trusted.

Related: `Week5_Deviations_From_Proposal.md` D5 (labels never human-verified), D7 (evaluation pool sizes), D8 (why a judge at all, and why on the control backbone). Implementation in the code repo: `src/metrics.py`.

---

## 1. The core measurement needs no language knowledge

The guardian task asks the judge to output one of two ASCII strings:

```
input  : <rules>{policy}</rules> + <transcript>{agent output}</transcript>
output : PASS  or  FAIL
```

Every UbuntuGuard row already carries that label. Scoring is therefore a comparison of two strings, and is entirely independent of what language the transcript is written in. The transcripts could be in a language nobody has ever seen and the arithmetic would be unchanged.

Evaluated on the held-out slice: **473 examples**, near-balanced at 246 PASS / 227 FAIL. Reported as **macro F1** rather than accuracy, so a judge that collapses onto the commoner label cannot score well.

Because we reuse the authors' system prompt verbatim, these numbers sit on the same scale as the ones their paper reports for Llama-3.3-70B, Qwen and Gemma.

## 2. What the measurement does *not* establish

The judge is scored against UbuntuGuard's labels, and those labels were generated by Llama-3.1-405B / Qwen3-235B under automated structural checks alone — no human ever confirmed that a FAIL transcript genuinely violates its policy (D5).

So a reported macro F1 of 0.80 means precisely:

> the judge agrees with those labels 80% of the time

and **not** "the judge is right 80% of the time". This distinction must survive into the paper, not be smoothed over.

Three reasons the study remains valid on that footing:

1. **It is the benchmark's definition.** Everyone who reports on UbuntuGuard, its own authors included, is scored against these labels. Our number is comparable to theirs whatever the labels' absolute quality.
2. **Label noise attenuates rather than fabricates.** Random noise in the labels shrinks measured differences; it does not manufacture them. If B4 beats B3 despite noisy labels, the true gap is at least the observed one. The bias runs against our own hypothesis.
3. **It is symmetric.** The same labels score every arm. A systematic labelling bias shifts B3 and B4 together, so the *difference* — which is what the result is — is protected even where the absolute level is not.

## 3. Four checks that can be run here, with no native speaker

### 3.1 The English control — the most informative one

337 questions appear in the English-only file and in **no** African file (D7), so nothing about them leaks through training on African data. English is readable here, so the judge's verdicts on this slice can be inspected by hand.

Two things come out of it:

- an upper bound on judge quality, verified rather than assumed;
- **the gap between English macro F1 and African macro F1**, which is itself a result. That gap is cross-lingual evaluator degradation, measured — the very phenomenon the project studies, appearing in our own instrument.

### 3.2 The majority-class floor

The evaluation slice is near-balanced, so always answering PASS scores about 52% accuracy and about 0.34 macro F1. Any judge not clearly above that has learned nothing. Costs nothing to report and settles the question before a reader raises it. Implemented as `majority_baseline`.

### 3.3 Per-language macro F1

Computed without reading any of the languages, and it determines **which languages the paper is allowed to make claims about**. If the judge scores 0.85 on Swahili and 0.55 on Nyanja, Nyanja drops out of the claims — justified by a number rather than by an apology.

`per_group_metrics` marks any group under 20 examples as `underpowered`. Given D7's pool (Nyanja 2 evaluation prompts, Luganda 7), several languages will carry that flag permanently.

### 3.4 Inter-judge agreement

Train two judges from different seeds and measure how often they agree. This needs **no reference labels at all**, so it is the one check untouched by D5. Low agreement means an unstable judge regardless of how either scores against the gold labels.

### 3.5 Extractor sensitivity

The authors' verdict extractor falls back, when no `<answer>` block is present, to returning FAIL if that word appears anywhere in the output. An output reading "no rules were violated, so this does not fail" scores FAIL — models that reason aloud are penalised against models that answer bluntly.

`extract_verdict` implements both that loose rule (for comparability) and a strict one returning UNKNOWN. **Report both.** The difference bounds how much of the score is an artefact of the extractor rather than of the model, and is worth stating precisely because the original does not.

## 4. What stays out of reach

We cannot establish that UbuntuGuard's labels are correct. That limitation is inherited from the benchmark and is declared, not worked around.

**Partial mitigation:** engage one native speaker for a single language — Hausa or Swahili are the most reachable — to check roughly 30 examples. This does not validate the dataset. It indicates whether the order of magnitude holds, and is to be described as a spot probe, never as validation.

## 5. Reporting template

Every downstream claim is bounded by the judge figures, so they belong in the results section, not an appendix.

| Quantity | Where from | Purpose |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Judge macro F1, African languages | 473-example held-out slice | headline reliability of the instrument |
| Judge macro F1, English | 337 contamination-free controls | verified upper bound |
| English − African gap | the two above | measured cross-lingual evaluator degradation |
| Majority-class floor | `majority_baseline` | the number any result must clear |
| Per-language macro F1 | `per_group_metrics` | which languages support a claim |
| Inter-judge agreement | two seeds | stability, independent of the gold labels |
| Loose vs strict extractor | both modes of `extract_verdict` | how much of the score is parsing artefact |

**Standing rule: no claim in the results may be stated more precisely than the judge's own measured reliability supports.**

## 6. Why this is worth writing up rather than hiding

The TukaBench paper reports that LLM-as-judge reliability degrades in low-resource languages, without quantifying it. The systematic review (arXiv:2608.14626) lists inadequate evaluation frameworks among the field's central failures, citing Vajjala (2025) that the problem is the evaluation and not only the models.

Measuring our own evaluator, per language, against a stated floor, with the extractor's contribution separated out, addresses that gap directly. The project's sharpest constraint — that its author cannot read the target languages — is what forces the instrument to be quantified instead of assumed, which is exactly what the field is said to be missing.
