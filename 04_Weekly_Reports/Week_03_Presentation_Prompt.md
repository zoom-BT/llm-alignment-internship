This is a required contract deliverable specifically about explaining alignment method families and their relevance to my future research project — so the content should be dominated by clear explanations, diagrams, and mathematical representations of the concepts themselves, not a chronological log of what I did. Do not enumerate the debugging process or list errors encountered, with one exception: keep the resource-constraint/OOM-related lessons (GPU memory limits, quantization hardware limits, quota exhaustion) since those are genuinely relevant findings about the methods themselves, not just implementation noise.

Context: Week 3 covers alignment and preference-learning methods (SFT, RLHF/PPO, RLOO, GRPO, DPO, ORPO, KTO, SimPO, RLAIF, Constitutional AI) and parameter-efficient fine-tuning (LoRA, QLoRA), building on Week 1-2's supervised fine-tuning work on Qwen2.5-0.5B with databricks-dolly-15k.

Structure:

**1. The landscape (1 slide, a diagram/taxonomy).** A tree or grid showing how alignment methods relate: feedback type (demonstrations / pairwise preferences / binary labels / AI feedback / rule-based) on one axis, whether a reference model and/or a separate reward model is required on another. Place SFT, RLHF/PPO, RLOO, GRPO, DPO, ORPO, KTO, SimPO, RLAIF, and Constitutional AI on this map. This sets up everything else.

**2. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning: LoRA (1-2 slides, needs a matrix diagram).** Explain the core idea: freeze the pretrained weight matrix `W` (shape d×k), add a trainable low-rank update `ΔW = B·A` where B is d×r, A is r×k, r≪min(d,k). Show the rank concept visually (a small worked example: a rank-1 matrix built from an outer product, showing it can't represent an arbitrary matrix — only a constrained family). Include the real numbers from my experiment: on Qwen2.5-0.5B's Q projection (896×896, r=16), LoRA uses 28,672 params vs. 802,816 full (~3.6%); across all Q/V projections in 24 layers, 1,081,344 trainable params total — 0.22% of the full 495M-parameter model, confirmed empirically via `print_trainable_parameters()`.

**3. QLoRA (1 slide).** Same LoRA adapter, but the frozen base is stored in 4-bit NF4 (NormalFloat4, designed for roughly-normally-distributed weights) plus double quantization (quantizing the per-block scaling constants too). Key resource-constraint finding to keep: bitsandbytes' 4-bit quantization is CUDA-only and has no CPU fallback at all — when Kaggle's GPU quota ran out mid-evaluation, this forced a workaround via Google Colab rather than falling back to CPU, which simply isn't possible for this method. Also note QLoRA was measurably slower per training step than plain LoRA (on-the-fly dequantization overhead) — a real time-for-memory tradeoff, not a free improvement.

**4. RLHF and PPO (1-2 slides, reuse the pipeline diagram + formulas).** The 3-stage pipeline (SFT → Reward Model → PPO), the Reward Model's Bradley-Terry loss (`loss(θ) = −E[log(σ(rθ(x,yw)−rθ(x,yl)))]`), and PPO's objective (`E[rθ(x,y) − β·log(π_RL/π_SFT)] + γ·E[log(π_RL(x))]`) with the KL-penalty and pretraining-mix terms explained. Frame PPO as actor-critic: it needs a learned value-function baseline in addition to the policy.

**5. Lighter-weight RL alternatives: REINFORCE → RLOO → GRPO (1-2 slides, a progression diagram).** Start from the policy gradient theorem (`∇J = E[∇log π(y|x)·R(x,y)]`) and its high-variance problem. Show baseline subtraction as the fix (`∇J = E[∇log π(y|x)·(R(x,y)−b(x))]`), proving briefly why it's unbiased (the baseline term's expectation is zero since probabilities sum to 1). Then show how each method gets its baseline differently: PPO learns one (a critic network); RLOO uses leave-one-out averaging across K samples of the same prompt (`b_i = (1/(K-1))·Σ_{j≠i} R(x,y_j)`, no critic needed); GRPO normalizes by the full group's mean *and* standard deviation instead. Emphasize: RLHF is the general framework, PPO/RLOO/GRPO are interchangeable optimization algorithms within it.

**6. DPO — deriving away the reward model (1-2 slides, the full substitution).** Show the closed-form optimal policy for the KL-regularized objective (`π*(y|x) = π_ref(y|x)·exp(r(x,y)/β)/Z(x)`), rearranged for r, substituted into the Bradley-Terry loss, with the `Z(x)` term canceling in the chosen-vs-rejected difference. End at the final DPO loss and note it's the *same* log-sigmoid structure as the reward model's own loss, just applied to a log-probability ratio instead of a trained reward.

**7. ORPO — one model, no reference (1 slide + the negative-result callout).** The odds-ratio loss (`odds_θ(y|x) = P_θ(y|x)/(1−P_θ(y|x))`), combined additively with a standard SFT term. No reference model needed at all — verified directly (no `ref_model` parameter exists in `ORPOTrainer`). **Keep this resource-constraint finding:** my own ORPO training diverged to NaN, and root-causing it took five targeted fixes, converging on a genuine hardware conflict: on a T4 GPU (which lacks full bf16 tensor-core support), fp16 training needs `GradScaler` for numerical stability, but `GradScaler` conflicts with `gradient_checkpointing` (a documented PyTorch issue) — and without `gradient_checkpointing`, the model didn't fit in the T4's 16GB even at batch_size=1. `GradScaler` and `gradient_checkpointing` turned out to be mutually exclusive requirements on this specific hardware/library pairing. Frame this as a legitimate negative result (per the internship agreement's Article 9), not a failure to hide.

**8. KTO and SimPO (1 slide, brief comparison, no deep derivation needed).** KTO: works on independently-labeled desirable/undesirable examples rather than paired comparisons, inspired by prospect theory (loss aversion). SimPO: reference-free like ORPO, but skips the SFT term too, using length-normalized sequence log-probability directly as the implicit reward plus a target margin.

**9. AI feedback: RLAIF and Constitutional AI (1 slide).** RLAIF = RLHF with an AI model replacing human labelers — cheaper to scale, but risks reproducing the judge model's own biases and blind spots. Constitutional AI: the model critiques and revises its own outputs against a written set of principles, and that revised data feeds SFT and/or preference-pair generation for RLAIF.

**10. Evaluators and their documented biases (1 slide).** LLM-as-judge's known failure modes: position bias (preferring whichever response is shown first/second), verbosity bias (favoring longer responses regardless of quality), self-preference bias, inconsistency across repeated calls. Also outcome vs. process reward models (scoring only the final answer vs. scoring each intermediate reasoning step).

**11. Alignment failure modes (1 slide, a short glossary with one-line definitions).** Sycophancy, excessive/insufficient refusal, preference overfitting, loss of response diversity, catastrophic forgetting/capability degradation.

**12. Results — grounding all of this in my own experiments (1-2 slides, a results table plus a bar chart).**
| Method | Perplexity (Dolly-15k test) |
|---|---|
| Baseline (no fine-tuning) | 13.92 |
| Week 1 full fine-tuning (broken hyperparameters) | 75.41 |
| Week 2 full fine-tuning (corrected) | 14.61 |
| LoRA (r=16) | **13.795 — best result of the project** |

Plus DPO's Rewards/accuracy improving from 55.4% to 61.9% over a small 1000-pair run, and ORPO's documented negative result. Key interpretation to state explicitly: LoRA's constrained update (0.22% of parameters) preserved general language-modeling ability better than full fine-tuning did — even after careful hyperparameter correction — a concrete, measured illustration of the "risk of forgetting" tradeoff the literature describes abstractly. QLoRA's compression cost real precision in exchange for a smaller memory footprint, a tradeoff that matters far more at larger model scales than at 0.5B.

**13. Relevance to the future research project (1 closing slide).** These method comparisons directly inform Week 4's topic scoping: LoRA is the practical default going forward (better result, smaller footprint, no quantization-hardware constraints); QLoRA and ORPO both surfaced real hardware/resource limits on a single T4 that any future experimental design needs to account for; RLHF's full pipeline (reward model + PPO) is likely out of reach at this project's compute budget, making DPO-family methods (DPO, and ORPO once the precision issue is resolved on better hardware) the realistic alignment approach for the actual research question.
